The bakery opened before the street did. At a quarter past four, Mara unlocked the back door, switched on the ovens, and stood for a moment in the dark listening to them tick as they warmed. The dough from the night before had risen under its damp cloths, slow and patient, the way the old baker who trained her had insisted it should. Fast dough tastes of nothing, he used to say. Give it the night.

By five the first trays were in. The smell of crust reached the front room and then, through the gap under the door, the pavement outside, where the first commuter of the day slowed down without quite knowing why. Mara shaped the rye loaves by hand, dusted them, scored them with three quick cuts, and slid them in beside the baguettes. The radio murmured the weather: rain by noon, clearing by evening.

Tomas arrived at six with the newspaper under his arm and the keys to the delivery van in his fist. He was the kind of man who announced himself by sighing. The van needs a new tyre, he said. The van always needs something, Mara said, and handed him a coffee and a roll still too hot to hold. He ate it anyway, shifting it from hand to hand, and read her the headlines while she filled the window: seeded rolls on the left, sweet things on the right, the big country loaves in the middle where people could see them from across the road.

At seven the bell over the door began to ring and did not stop until nine. The regulars came in an order so fixed that Mara could have set the clock by them. The teacher who bought two croissants and ate one on the way to school. The nurse coming off a night shift who wanted only a slice of the dark bread with butter and would not sit down. The boy who was sent for the family's loaf every morning and every morning forgot the change, so that his mother came by in the afternoon, apologising, and bought a cake she had not meant to buy.

Between customers Mara kneaded the next batch. Her hands knew the work and left her mind free to wander. She thought about the rent, which went up each spring like the river, and about the new place that had opened by the station selling bread that had been frozen somewhere far away and finished in an oven the size of a wardrobe. She thought about her daughter, who was studying in another city and sent photographs of other people's bakeries with the message: not as good as yours.

The rain came as the radio had promised, and the shop filled with wet coats and the particular cheerfulness of people who have escaped something. Tomas came back from his round soaked and triumphant with the news that the hotel on the hill wanted forty loaves a day from next month. Forty, Mara said. Forty, he said. She did the arithmetic in flour on the counter and wiped it away before anyone could see her smile.

By two the window was almost empty. Mara swept, counted the till, and set the dough for the next morning, folding it over itself and covering it with the damp cloths. Give it the night. She turned off the lights in the front and sat for a while in the warm back room with the last roll of the day and a cup of coffee that had gone cold hours ago, listening to the rain ease and the ovens tick as they cooled.
